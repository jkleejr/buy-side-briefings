summary: Every transformative technology of the last two centuries — railways, radio, the internet — produced a stock bubble, and in nearly every case the technology delivered anyway while a specific group of investors got wiped out. The pattern is consistent enough to be useful. Today's AI market rhymes most with 1920s radio and 1990s telecom infrastructure, least with dot-com valuations. History's clearest lessons — don't try to time the exit, don't confuse the technology winning with today's leaders' shareholders winning, and respect the top-of-index curse — are all actionable now.

## Why this report exists

The AI trade dominates the market: the Magnificent 7 are roughly a third of the S&P 500, Nvidia alone is ~8%, and four companies plan ~$725B of capex this year. The natural question is *what usually happens next* — and it turns out the historical record is deep, quantified, and surprisingly consistent. This report documents each major technology boom-bust with numbers, extracts base rates from ~150 years of crashes, and ends with scenarios for the AI era and what each implies. Where a report on today's evidence would cover the present cycle, this one covers everything that came before it.

A core source throughout is a paper we reviewed in full: [Sorescu et al., *Two Centuries of Innovations and Stock Market Bubbles* (Marketing Science, 2018)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2018.1095) — 51 major innovations from 1825–2000, formal econometric bubble tests, and the single most useful finding in this whole literature: **bubbles formed around 37 of the 51 (73%), and yet portfolios of the innovating firms beat the market by an average of +44.6 points from each bubble's beginning to its end.** The bubble is normal. The technology usually delivers. The people who get hurt are specific and identifiable — and they're mostly the ones who buy nearest the peak.

## The pattern, episode by episode

### UK Railway Mania (1840s) — the concentration record-holder

- **Run-up:** railway shares went from [23% to 71% of the entire UK stock market between 1838 and 1846](https://www.techpolicy.press/lessons-for-the-ai-bubble-from-three-historic-crashes/) — a concentration that makes today's Mag 7 at ~34% look moderate. At the 1847 peak, railway investment consumed roughly [7% of British GDP](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1537338), more than twice the military budget. Britons of every class — clergymen, widows, shopkeepers — piled in.
- **Crash:** the railway share index fell [~64% from its July 1845 peak to the October 1849 trough](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1537338), a four-year grind with only minor rebounds. Investor losses were roughly £80M of £250M invested — scaled to the modern US economy, [about $2.3 trillion of losses on $7 trillion invested](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1537338).
- **What actually broke:** not the technology — execution math. Construction costs came in ~50% over budget, operating costs ate 50% of revenue instead of the projected 40%, and revenues ran 30–40% below projections, on projects that only promised ~7% returns to begin with. Small misses, levered structure, huge loss.
- **Long term:** the technology over-delivered. Hardly any lines went bankrupt; by 1905 railway revenues hit 6% of GDP — *more* than the mania-era forecasts implied. But the benefits went to users and the broader economy, and within the capital structure, bondholders and preference shareholders did fine while **common shareholders ate nearly all the losses.** The mania built Britain's industrial backbone with other people's money.

### Radio and the 1929 crash — the closest rhyme to AI

- **Run-up:** RCA — the Nvidia of the 1920s — rose roughly [200-fold across the decade](https://finaeon.com/rca-and-the-roaring-twenties/), riding a genuinely explosive technology: US radio stations grew from 5 to 556 in two years, and radio sales grew 14x from 1922 to 1929. RCA was a real company with real revenue and a near-monopoly position.
- **Crash:** RCA fell [~98% peak-to-trough](https://finaeon.com/rca-and-the-roaring-twenties/) (September 1929 to May 1932). The Dow fell 89%; the S&P didn't see a deeper bear before or since ([-83% on Yardeni's S&P series](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/indices/equity/sp-500/)). Pure "echo" stocks — companies with radio in the name and nothing behind it, like Kolster Radio (10 → 95 → below 1, bankrupt) — went to zero.
- **Recovery:** the Dow didn't reclaim its 1929 *nominal* peak until 1954 — 25 years. (In inflation-adjusted terms, deflation and dividends made it [closer to 7 years](https://www.morningstar.com/economy/what-weve-learned-150-years-stock-market-crashes), a reminder that the scariest recovery numbers are usually the nominal ones.) RCA itself never durably traded above its 1929 price again as an independent company — it recovered into the 1960s on *television* (the next technology), stalled after 1967, and was bought by GE in 1986.
- **The lesson:** radio changed the world exactly as promised. The 1929 buyer of the best radio company still waited ~30 years to break even. **A real technology and a ~98% drawdown in its flagship stock are fully compatible.**

### The Nifty Fifty (1972–74) — what happens to "one-decision" quality stocks

- **Run-up:** the fifty "one-decision" great American growth companies traded at [42x earnings at the 1972 peak, vs 19x for the S&P](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nifty_Fifty). No fraud, no vaporware — Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Disney, Xerox.
- **Crash:** the S&P fell 48% over 21 months (1973–74). The quality names fell harder: Coca-Cola –69%, Xerox –71%, McDonald's –72%, Disney –87%, Polaroid –91%. The Dow's January 1973 high wasn't surpassed until November 1982; in *real* terms the market didn't recover until [August 1993 — twenty years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973%E2%80%931974_stock_market_crash).
- **The famous twist (Jeremy Siegel's finding):** an investor who bought the Nifty Fifty *at the exact 1972 peak* and held for 25 years still matched the S&P 500 (~13% annually), because the underlying businesses grew earnings ~11%/year the whole way. Overpaying for durable quality was survivable — painfully, over decades. Overpaying for fragile stories was not.

### Dot-com and telecom (1995–2002) — two different bubbles in one

- **Run-up:** NASDAQ rose ~800% from 1995 to its March 2000 peak of 5,048, reaching a [P/E of ~200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-com_bubble) (Japan's infamous 1989 bubble peaked near 80). Cisco traded at ~472x earnings. Qualcomm rose 2,619% in 1999 alone.
- **Crash:** NASDAQ –78% by October 2002; ~$5 trillion erased; the S&P fell 49% over 929 days. Only about half of the dot-coms founded in the late '90s still existed by 2004.
- **The telecom half is the part that rhymes with AI:** equipment and carrier companies buried [$500B+ of mostly debt-financed fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecoms_crash); bond investors recovered barely 20 cents on the dollar; Lucent and Nortel — who financed their own customers' purchases — were destroyed.
- **Recovery and who won:** the S&P took ~7 years to reclaim its 2000 peak, then fell again in 2008 — a buyer at the 2000 top was up only [~69% in real total-return terms after 18 years](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/dshort/updates/2023/01/03/the-four-totally-bad-bears-recoveries-compared). NASDAQ needed 15 years. Cisco — the "arms dealer" everyone called safe — never reclaimed its 2000 price in the following quarter-century. Microsoft took ~17 years. Amazon fell 94%... and then returned 100x for whoever held or bought the wreckage. Meanwhile the actual winners of the internet — Google (IPO 2004), Facebook (2012) — **didn't exist as public companies during the bubble.** The internet delivered beyond the wildest 1999 forecasts; almost none of the 1999 portfolio captured it.

## The base rates — 150 years of crashes in one place

| Measure | The number |
| --- | --- |
| Bear market frequency | ~19 real (inflation-adjusted) bears in 150 years — [roughly one per decade](https://www.morningstar.com/economy/what-weve-learned-150-years-stock-market-crashes); nominal 20%+ declines every ~3.5–5 years |
| Average bear | [–35% over ~9.6 months](https://www.hartfordfunds.com/practice-management/client-conversations/managing-emotions/bear-markets.html); average bull: +112% over ~2.7 years |
| Bears with a recession | [–43% average, ~10.75 years to full recovery](https://www.invesco.com/content/dam/invesco/emea/en/pdf/T_con_zero-bear_necessities_apr_2020.pdf); without a recession: –27%, whole episode over in ~1.5 years |
| Time to reclaim peak (worst cases) | 1929: 25yr nominal · 1973: 8yr nominal, ~20yr real · 2000: 7–9yr, "lost decade" through 2013 real · 2008: 5.5yr · 1987: 1.7yr · 2020: 5 months |
| How often stocks rise | ~75% of rolling 12-month periods; ~3 of 4 calendar years |
| The long game | $1 in US stocks in 1871 → [$35,082 by 2026, after inflation, through all 19 crashes](https://www.morningstar.com/economy/what-weve-learned-150-years-stock-market-crashes) |

Three base rates deserve special attention for an AI-heavy market:

**1. The top-of-index curse is real — but it took the last decade off.** Since 1957, the 10 largest S&P stocks have lagged the other 490 by [~2.4 points per year](https://www.gmo.com/americas/research-library/magnificently-concentrated_insights/); across every G-8 country and every horizon tested, sector "top dogs" underperformed their own sector, by 5.3 points in the following year on average ([Arnott, "Too Big to Succeed"](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/publications/articles)). The huge caveat: 2014–2024 inverted this — the top 10 *outperformed* by ~4.9 points/year. Either "it's different now" (scale advantages, network effects) or the mean reversion is deferred and loaded. GMO's testable 2024 call: cap-weighted underperforms equal-weight over the next decade.

**2. Valuation predicts decade-ahead returns, not crash timing.** Shiller CAPE above 40 — [first time since the dot-com era](https://www.reuters.com/markets/) — has historically meant below-average 10-year returns, and properly-constructed CAPE explains [half the variance in 10-year outcomes](https://papers.ssrn.com/). But it says nothing about the next 12 months, and since 1964 even the *worst* 10-year S&P outcome was only about –1.3%/year. High valuations argue for tempered expectations, not for exit.

**3. Timing costs more than crashes do.** Missing the 10 best days over 1996–2025 [cut ending wealth by 56%](https://www.hartfordfunds.com/practice-management/client-conversations/managing-emotions/timing-market.html) — and 76% of those best days happened *during* bear markets or the first two months of new bulls, exactly when a timer is out. Schwab's 20-year study: a *perfect* timer beat an invest-immediately investor by only ~$700/year, while even the world's *worst* timer (buying every annual peak) tripled the perpetual cash-holder. Buying at all-time highs has historically produced *better* 1–5 year forward returns than average days. The honest academic caveat: timing costs are roughly symmetric (missing the worst days helps about as much), so the real argument isn't "never touch anything" — it's that nobody reliably picks which days, and the base-rate bet is staying in.

## The frameworks that organize all this

**Sorescu et al. (2018) — bubbles are a feature of how markets finance radical technology.** Bubble magnitude rises with an innovation's radicalness, network effects, and public visibility (AI maxes all three, so this framework *predicts* today's bubble is among the largest ever). Bubbles led adoption takeoff by ~4.5 years on average; the cheap capital raised during them funded the buildout; and they showed **no statistical link to interest rates or money supply** — innovation bubbles are intrinsic to the technology, not artifacts of the Fed. And through all of it, bubble-start-to-bubble-end holders of the innovators beat the market.

**Carlota Perez — installation, frenzy, turning point, deployment.** Each ~50-year technological revolution runs a speculative "installation" phase that ends in a crash, followed by a "deployment" golden age where the technology actually transforms the economy — the 1847 railway crash and 1929 both mark that turning point. On this reading, [AI is in the frenzy phase](https://www.techpolicy.press/) of an installation that began with deep learning in the early 2010s. Perez implies the crash is *part of the process* — and that the biggest economic payoff comes after it.

**Capital cycle theory:** returns follow supply, not demand; the flood → boom → collapse → inheritance sequence played out in railways, fiber, and shale; losses concentrate where leveraged capacity was built (today: neoclouds, GPU-backed debt, power).

## What 2026 most and least resembles

**Most resembles — 1920s radio.** A genuinely revolutionary technology; profitable, dominant flagship companies (RCA then, Nvidia now); explosive real adoption running alongside speculative echo stocks; bubble-magnitude predictors (radical + network effects + visibility) all maxed. The radio lesson: the technology delivering didn't protect the peak-buyer of even the best company.

**Second-closest — 1990s telecom (not dot-com).** The infrastructure half: hundreds of billions of debt-financed buildout, vendor financing (Lucent/Nortel then, Nvidia's $110B ecosystem book and GPU-collateralized private credit now), and collateral whose value depends on the boom continuing. This is where the 20-cents-on-the-dollar outcomes lived.

**Least resembles — dot-com equity valuations.** The Mag 7 trade around [26–28x forward earnings vs ~66x for 1999's top seven](https://www.cresset.com/); Cisco hit 472x, Nvidia is ~25x forward with 53% net margins and ~$99B of trailing profit. Hyperscaler capex-to-free-cash-flow is below 1.0 vs ~4x at the 2000 telecom peak. And prior bubble bursts (1929, 2000, 2008) all came during Fed *tightening*; 2024–26 is an easing cycle. The froth is real, but it is froth on top of enormous actual earnings — which is why the correction math looks more like the Nifty Fifty than like pets.com.

**Wildcard the history doesn't cover:** every prior episode was mostly *equity*-financed, so losses landed on shareholders who knowingly took equity risk. The AI buildout is increasingly funded by debt, SPVs, and private credit — the loss-distribution machinery of 2008 more than of 2000. That's the genuinely novel risk, and it's why the credit signals are the ones worth tracking.

## Scenarios — grounded in the base rates

Rough subjective probabilities, anchored to how often each shape has actually occurred after comparable setups:

**1. Selective correction / rolling AI bear — ~40%.** The non-recessionary base case: sector-concentrated drawdown of 25–35%, high-multiple and leveraged names (neoclouds, AI-adjacent story stocks) fall 50–80%, profitable leaders fall 30–50% and recover within ~2 years as earnings grow into valuations. Precedent: Nifty Fifty names, 2022's –25%. Arguably began in June 2026 (KOSPI circuit breaker, Oracle's worst week since 2002, Broadcom's $300B day). For an index holder this is painful but routine; for leveraged single names it's terminal.

**2. Full bust, dot-com shape — ~25%.** Index –45–55%, AI complex –70%+, recession involved (recessionary bears average –43%, ~10 years to full recovery), the $2T funding gap and private-credit plumbing turn a market event into a credit event. The technology keeps deploying through the wreckage; the "Googles of AI" emerge afterward, possibly companies that don't exist publicly today. Dry powder and second movers inherit.

**3. Melt-up first, worse bust later — ~20%.** Perez's frenzy isn't finished: easing Fed + fear-of-missing-out push concentration and valuations toward genuine 1999 levels (Mag 7 at 40x+, capex past $1T/yr) before the break. The most dangerous scenario for someone who de-risks early *and* for someone who chases late — the 1928 problem, where the last year up was enormous and the give-back was bigger.

**4. Smooth deployment, no bust — ~15%.** Revenue catches the capex (the Microsoft $37B-run-rate / Anthropic 30x-in-15-months trajectory continues), valuations digest sideways, and the market grinds through like 1950s electrification or 1995's mid-cycle pause. The rarest historical shape — transformative technologies at maximum visibility have almost never avoided their crash — but real profits and sub-30x multiples make it more plausible than at any prior frenzy peak.

## What history actually instructs

1. **Expect the bubble; don't let it evict you.** 73% of major innovations bubbled, the market rises three years out of four, and the timer's edge over the holder was ~$700/year even with perfect foresight. The base-rate move is to stay invested and size so that a –35% average bear (or a –50% tail) is survivable without forced selling.
2. **Separate "AI wins" from "my AI stocks win."** Radio won and RCA's 1929 buyers waited 30 years; the internet won and Cisco's 2000 buyers never got back; the internet's real winners IPO'd *after* the crash. If you hold AI single names, know which claim you own: profitable arms dealer, leveraged builder, or echo stock. History treats them completely differently — the middle category (debt-financed capacity: the neocloud model) is where 20-cents-on-the-dollar outcomes have lived in every cycle.
3. **Respect the concentration math.** A cap-weighted index fund is now a ~34% bet on seven stocks whose class has historically lagged by 2.4 points/year — a headwind, not a catastrophe. Owning some equal-weight, international, or value exposure is how an index holder takes the other side of the top-of-index curse without timing anything.
4. **Quality bought expensive is slow; junk bought expensive is fatal.** The Nifty Fifty at 42x matched the market over 25 years. Kolster Radio and pets.com went to zero. If you overpay, overpay for real earnings.
5. **The inheritance phase is a plan, not a slogan.** In every episode the biggest fortunes were made buying built infrastructure and surviving franchises *after* the collapse — the assets outlive their financiers. Keeping some dry powder and a shopping list is a position.
6. **Watch the turn signals, not the noise:** the first hyperscaler capex *cut*; credit stress in GPU-backed/private-credit paper; an AI IPO flood and share-funded M&A binge; buyback halts; depreciation schedules shortening.

## Honest limits of this report

History gives base rates, not forecasts — four great bears produced wildly divergent 18-year outcomes, which is itself the strongest argument against using any single analog as a template. Survivorship bias runs through the whole literature (we study the innovations that worked). And the one structural novelty of this cycle — debt and private credit in place of equity — means the loss distribution if it breaks may look less like 2000 (shareholders lose) and more like a credit cycle (it spreads). The verified per-claim sourcing is thinner than our usual standard on a few numbers where the checking infrastructure rate-limited; the load-bearing figures (drawdowns, recovery times, base rates) are corroborated across at least two independent sources in our set or the Sorescu primary paper.
